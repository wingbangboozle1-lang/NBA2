Skill vs luck — is any of this better than backing the favourite?

Every accuracy figure elsewhere on this site answers the wrong question. "Our side hit 64.7% of 167 games" sounds like skill until you ask whether the side we picked was simply the better team. This page answers the right question with a paired test on exactly the games where our model and the naive rule disagree — because those are the only games that carry any information about skill at all.

The result, before the detail. Backing the team with the better prior win record scored 70.3% on 148 of the same 167 games. Our model scored 64.7%. On the 33 games where the two disagreed, the naive rule was right 20 times and the model 13 times (exact McNemar p = 0.2962). The model has therefore demonstrated no skill that is separable from the favourite's win rate at this sample size. That is a finding, not a bug, and it is exactly what the brief's "remove luck from the equation" step is for.

1. The naive baselines, measured on the same games

Each baseline consumes only information that existed before its game tipped — prior results, nothing else — and is scored on the identical set of regular-season games the walk-forward model scored. Ties are skipped and the skipped count is published rather than hidden.

2. The paired test — McNemar on the disagreements

A paired comparison does not need 300 games, because only the discordant games carry information: if our model and the naive rule pick the same side, that game tells us nothing about which is better. McNemar's exact test asks whether the split of the discordant games is consistent with a coin flip.

S1 backs the side its own ratings favour, and its ratings are built from the same results a reader can see. So the honest question is not 'is 64.7% better than 50%' - of course it is, favourites win - but 'is it better than simply backing the favourite'. The discordant-game test answers that, and it needs far fewer games than the 300-bet bar because only the disagreements carry information.

3. Why the headline hit rate is high, and why that is not the same as skill

The model's side is the side its own ratings favour, and those ratings are built from the same box scores a reader can look at. Naturally it picks the better team most of the time, and naturally the better team wins more than half the time. The high hit rate is inherited from the favourite's base rate; the discordant test above subtracts that inheritance and finds nothing left over.

This is why the site refuses to promote a strategy on accuracy alone. Two further measurements tell the same story:

  • Sample size. The largest strategy has 167 settled bets against a 300-bet promotion bar (gate G7).
  • Confidence intervals. Every rate on this site is published with a Wilson interval, and every interval here is wide enough to contain the base rate. A wide interval is not a verdict; it is the honest absence of one.

4. What measurement would settle it

The route out is arithmetic, not opinion: more discordant games, and a market price to test against.

What this page does not say. It does not say the model is worse than a naive rule — the point estimate is that the naive rule is ahead on the disagreement set, with a p of 0.30 on 33 games, which is not evidence either way. It says the model has not yet earned the claim, and that publishing the claim without this test would be the hallucination the brief forbids.
